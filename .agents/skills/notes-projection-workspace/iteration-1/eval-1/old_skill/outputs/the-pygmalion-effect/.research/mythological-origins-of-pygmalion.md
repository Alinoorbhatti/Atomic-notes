# Deep Research: Mythological Origins of Pygmalion

## Overview of the Myth
In Book X of Ovid’s *Metamorphoses*, Pygmalion is a sculptor from Cyprus who becomes disgusted by the immoral behavior of local women (specifically the Propoetides, whom Venus punished for denying her divinity). Resolving to live a celibate life, he uses his artistic talent to carve an ivory statue of a woman so flawless and realistic that he falls in love with it. He names her (later tradition calls her Galatea, though Ovid never names her) and treats her as a real partner, dressing her in clothes and bringing her gifts.

During the festival of Venus, the goddess of love, Pygmalion makes an offering at the altar. Under the smoking incense and gilded horns of the sacrificed heifers, he prays for a wife who is the "living likeness" of his ivory creation. Venus, sensing his true desire, makes the altar fire flare up three times. When Pygmalion returns home and kisses the statue, he finds that it grows warm and soft under his touch, transforming into a real, breathing woman. They marry with Venus's blessing and have a child named Paphos.

## Psychological Metaphor and the Pygmalion Effect
The myth has long been analyzed as a powerful metaphor for the way belief and expectation can shape reality. In 1968, Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson published their seminal study, *Pygmalion in the Classroom*, which demonstrated that teachers' expectations of students' intellectual growth acted as a self-fulfilling prophecy. When teachers expected certain students to perform better (even though those students were selected at random), their expectations influenced their own teaching behavior, ultimately causing those students to show actual gains in IQ and academic performance.

### Key Conceptual Parallels:
1. **Self-Fulfilling Prophecy**: The core of the Pygmalion effect is that expectations (positive or negative) lead to actions that make the expectation come true. Pygmalion's belief and dedication to his ideal woman eventually resulted in her coming to life.
2. **The Role of the "Creator"**: Teachers or managers act as the "sculptor," and their students or employees represent the "statue." Through attention, encouragement, and high standards, they shape the potential of the subject.
3. **Ambivalence and Projection**: In modern psychological analysis, the myth also warns against the obsession with perfection and the danger of projecting one's ideals onto another. It highlights how Pygmalion preferred a controlled, silent fantasy over the realities of human relationships.
