# Rosenthal-Jacobson Classroom Study

The **Rosenthal-Jacobson Classroom Study** (also known as the Oak School experiment) is a landmark 1968 social psychology study conducted by Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson. It demonstrated the **Pygmalion Effect**, a phenomenon where an authority figure's high expectations of an individual lead to improved performance, acting as a self-fulfilling prophecy.

## Methodology

The study was conducted at "Oak School," a public elementary school in a lower-class community. 
1. **The Assessment:** All students (grades 1–6) were administered a standard, non-verbal intelligence test (the Test of General Ability, or TOGA). However, teachers were told the test was the "Harvard Test of Inflected Acquisition," a specialized tool designed to identify students poised for sudden, dramatic cognitive development ("intellectual bloomers").
2. **Random Selection:** The researchers ignored the actual test scores. They randomly designated approximately 20% of the students in each classroom as the experimental group ("bloomers") and the remaining 80% as the control group.
3. **The Experimental Manipulation:** The teachers were provided with the names of the "bloomers." The only difference between the experimental and control groups was the teachers' belief in the bloomers' potential.
4. **Follow-up:** The students were retested with the same IQ test at the end of the school year, and also after one and two years to track long-term gains.

## Results

At the end of the first year:
- **Significant IQ Gains:** The randomly labeled "bloomers" showed significantly greater intellectual growth and higher IQ gains compared to the control group.
- **Developmental Differences:** The effect was strongest in the younger grades. In Grade 1, bloomers gained an average of 27.4 IQ points (compared to 12.0 in the control group); in Grade 2, bloomers gained 16.5 IQ points (compared to 7.0 in the control group).
- **Teacher Perceptions:** Teachers rated the bloomers as more appealing, better adjusted, more affectionate, and more likely to succeed.

## The Four-Factor Theory (Rosenthal, 1973)

To explain how teacher expectations are transmitted into student outcomes, Rosenthal proposed a four-factor theory:

*   **Rosenthal Climate Factor:** Teachers create a warmer socio-emotional environment for favored students through non-verbal cues (e.g., smiling, nodding, leaning in, warmer vocal tone).
*   **Rosenthal Input Factor:** Teachers teach more material and offer higher-quality, more challenging content to students they expect to succeed.
*   **Rosenthal Output Factor:** Teachers give high-expectation students more opportunities to respond, such as calling on them more frequently, letting them talk longer, and encouraging active participation.
*   **Rosenthal Feedback Factor:** Teachers provide more detailed, constructive, and helpful feedback to favored students, including praise for correct answers and detailed guidance on how to correct mistakes.

## Methodological Critiques

Despite its influence, the study faced severe criticism from psychometricians and educational psychologists:

1. **Flawed Testing Instrument:** Robert Thorndike pointed out that the TOGA was highly unsuitable for young children. The pre-test scores for some first-grade classes were absurdly low (indicating intellectual disability), meaning any subsequent "gains" were likely statistical artifacts.
2. **Regression to the Mean:** Because the baseline scores were aberrantly low due to test difficulty or confusion, the students' scores naturally moved back toward the average on the post-test (regression to the mean), not because of teacher expectation.
3. **Data Inconsistencies:** Richard Snow critiqued the study's data handling, pointing to high rates of attrition (students who weren't retested) and wild variations in reported effect sizes across publications. He also noted that many teachers could not recall which students had been designated as "bloomers."

## Sociological and Educational Significance

In sociology, the study is a critical reference point for:
- **Labeling Theory:** Showing how social labels attached by authority figures become internalized and modify an individual's identity and behavior.
- **Teacher Expectations and Student Tracking:** Underlining how institutional streaming and grouping create systemic inequalities. Teachers assigned to lower tracks hold lower expectations, adjusting their Climate, Input, Output, and Feedback accordingly, which institutionalizes student underachievement.

## Key Figures Mentioned

*   **Robert Rosenthal:** Co-author of the 1968 study and developer of the four-factor theory.
*   **Lenore Jacobson:** Principal of Oak School and co-author of the study, who provided access and practical school context.
*   **Robert Thorndike:** Leading educational psychologist who published a scathing psychometric critique of the study's test validity.
*   **Richard Snow:** Stanford educational psychologist who conducted deep methodological audits of the study design and data.
